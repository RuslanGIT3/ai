# The Psychology of Winning the Lottery

There's a version of this story you already know. Someone buys a two-dollar ticket on the way home from work, the numbers line up, and every problem they've ever had dissolves overnight. No more rent. No more boss. No more checking the balance before buying groceries. And there's a second version you've also heard: the cursed winner, broke within a few years, family torn apart, wishing they had never bought the ticket. Both stories feel true. Both get repeated constantly. And the best research we have says neither of them is quite right. What actually happens inside a person's head when they suddenly become rich is stranger than the fairy tale and stranger than the curse, and once you understand it, you understand something about money, and about your own brain, that has very little to do with lotteries.

Start with the curse, because that's the version most people believe now. You've probably seen the statistic: seventy percent of lottery winners go broke within a few years. It shows up in finance articles, in seminars, in videos exactly like this one. It's almost always credited to the National Endowment for Financial Education. In 2018, that organization published a statement saying the number was not backed by its research and that it could not confirm it. As far as anyone has been able to trace, the most quoted fact about lottery winners has no study behind it at all.

So are winners actually fine? Not exactly. The real evidence doesn't say winners are doomed, and it doesn't say they live happily ever after. It says something much more specific: money changes certain parts of a person very reliably, and leaves other parts almost completely untouched. The trouble starts when people confuse the two.

To see where that line runs, we have to go back to 1978.

That year, three psychologists, Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman, published a study that would be cited for decades. They tracked down twenty-two people who had won major prizes in the Illinois State Lottery, fifty thousand dollars or more, which was a life-changing sum at the time. They compared them with ordinary people from the same area who hadn't won anything. And for a third group, they interviewed people who had recently been paralyzed in accidents.

The obvious prediction is that the winners would be far happier than everyone else. They weren't. When asked how happy they were, the winners' answers were not significantly different from the control group's. And on one measure, they actually did worse. The researchers asked everyone how much pleasure they got from small, ordinary things: talking with a friend, watching television, eating breakfast, hearing a funny joke, getting a compliment. The winners reported getting less pleasure from those everyday moments than the people who hadn't won.

That second finding is the important one, because it points to the mechanism. The authors described two forces working against the winner. The first is contrast. Once you've felt the peak of watching your numbers come up, a nice breakfast is measured against that moment, and a nice breakfast loses. The second is habituation. The new house, the new car, the freedom from bills, all of it feels incredible at first, and then it simply becomes the background of your life. The thrill was real. It just had a short half-life.

Psychologists later gave this a name: hedonic adaptation. In plain terms, your brain is built to notice changes, not states. A raise feels good for a few weeks. Then it's just your salary. A new phone feels special for a few days. Then it's just your phone. The lottery is the most extreme version of this, because it's the biggest, fastest change most people could ever experience. And the brain does the same thing with it that it does with everything else. It adjusts.

There's a second reason the fantasy overshoots, and it happens before anyone wins anything. Psychologists who study what they call affective forecasting, our predictions about how future events will make us feel, have found that people are consistently bad at it. We overestimate how intense a feeling will be and how long it will last. Daniel Kahneman described one version of this as the focusing illusion, and he summed it up in a single line: nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it.

When you imagine winning the lottery, you picture the moment of freedom: quitting the job, paying off the house, the look on your family's face. What you don't picture is the next eleven thousand ordinary days, which will still contain traffic, colds, arguments, boredom and bad sleep. The fantasy is a highlight reel. Real life is the full broadcast. And because the brain plans around the highlight reel, it expects the money to deliver a feeling that no amount of money has ever been able to deliver for very long.

So that's the end of the story, right? Money doesn't buy happiness, case closed. Here's the problem. That 1978 study had twenty-two winners in it. Nearly half of the winners the researchers approached didn't take part. It was a small, early study, and for forty years it carried far more weight than a sample that size should carry. If you wanted a real answer, you would need thousands of winners, followed for years, compared with people who were similar to them in every way except the win.

In 2020, three economists, Erik Lindqvist, Robert Östling and David Cesarini, published almost exactly that study. They used Swedish lotteries, where records are good and winners can be followed over time, and they surveyed a large sample of players five to twenty-two years after a major lottery event. Because winning is random, the comparison is unusually clean: the only systematic difference between a winner and a similar non-winner is the money.

Their main result contradicts the classic story. Compared with matched controls, people who won large prizes reported higher overall life satisfaction, and that increase lasted for more than a decade with no sign of fading. So the treadmill doesn't erase everything. Something real and durable changes.

But look at what changed, because this is the most counterintuitive part of the entire topic. The researchers measured several different things. When they asked about life satisfaction, which means how you evaluate your life when you step back and judge it as a whole, winners were clearly better off. When they looked at happiness and mental health, the day-to-day emotional experience, the effects were much smaller. Their follow-up analysis pointed mostly toward financial satisfaction as the driver. People felt better about their financial situation, and that lifted how they rated their lives.

Put simply, the money changed how winners judged their lives far more than it changed how their days felt. It looks like a happiness machine. Psychologically, it works more like a security machine. It removes a specific kind of worry, the worry about bills, about emergencies, about whether you'll be okay. That's genuinely valuable. It's also not the same thing as feeling joyful on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon.

And that gap, between what people expect money to fix and what it actually fixes, is where most of the real damage happens. Because if winning doesn't automatically make people miserable, why do so many winner stories end badly? To answer that, we need to stop looking at feelings and start looking at behavior.

In 2011, economists Scott Hankins, Mark Hoekstra and Paige Marta Skiba published a study of Florida lottery winners linked to bankruptcy records. They compared people who won between fifty thousand and a hundred and fifty thousand dollars with people who won less than ten thousand. These were people who had all been playing, so they were similar to begin with. The only real difference was the size of the prize.

You would expect the bigger winners to be protected. In the short run, they were: in the first couple of years, they were noticeably less likely to file for bankruptcy. But three to five years after the win, the advantage was gone. The large winners were filing at roughly the same rates as the small winners. The researchers' conclusion was blunt: the money postponed bankruptcy, it did not prevent it. They also found that the big winners didn't use the prize to pay down their debt or build up assets in any lasting way.

How is that possible? A hundred thousand dollars should be enough to wipe out a lot of debt. The answer is a concept from behavioral economics called mental accounting, an idea associated with Richard Thaler. In plain language: your brain doesn't treat all money as the same money. Money you earned through work goes into one mental box, labeled careful. Money that falls from the sky goes into a different box, labeled fun. A gambler who's up at a casino calls their winnings house money and bets it more freely, even though a dollar won is exactly as valuable as a dollar earned.

A lottery prize is the purest house money there is. It doesn't feel like yours in the same way your paycheck does, so it's easier to spend, easier to give away, easier to gamble with. And here's the trap that sits underneath. The prize arrives once. But a lot of what people buy with it doesn't cost money once. A bigger house means a bigger mortgage or bigger property taxes, bigger insurance, bigger maintenance. A new car means higher insurance and upkeep. A new lifestyle means new recurring costs. The winner has converted a one-time payment into a permanent increase in spending. For a while, the numbers look healthy. The bank balance is bigger than it's ever been. But the balance is a snapshot. The monthly flow underneath is quietly going the wrong way.

That's the pattern behind many of the stories you've heard. It isn't that winners are stupid. It's that the human brain is very good at noticing a big pile of money and very bad at noticing a slow leak.

And for the very largest wins, there's a second illusion that starts before the money even arrives: the headline number. When a jackpot is advertised as three hundred million dollars, that's usually the total of a long series of payments over decades. Take the lump sum instead, as most big winners do, and the figure drops sharply. Then come taxes. We'll see a real example in a moment where an advertised jackpot of over three hundred million turned into a little over a hundred and thirteen million in the bank. That's still an enormous amount of money. But the winner's brain has already anchored on the first number. Psychologically, they feel richer than they are, and they start making promises sized to the headline, not to the bank balance.

So far we've talked about what happens inside the winner. But money doesn't stay inside one person. The moment a win becomes known, it changes every relationship around them, and this is where the chain reaction really begins.

Think about what a sudden fortune does to a normal friendship. Before, if a friend asked to borrow a few hundred dollars, it was a favor. Now, if a cousin asks for help with a medical bill, a friend asks for a business loan, an old classmate asks for a down payment, every one of those requests is technically affordable. Saying no no longer means I can't. It means I won't. And that turns every refusal into a judgment about the relationship itself. Some therapists who work with newly wealthy clients use the term sudden wealth syndrome for the mix of guilt, isolation, suspicion and identity confusion that can follow. It isn't a formal medical diagnosis, but the pattern they describe is consistent: people stop knowing who likes them for themselves, and they begin to withdraw.

That withdrawal makes sense from the inside. It also removes the very people who might have told them they were making a mistake.

And the ripple doesn't stop with friends and family. In 2019, economists Sumit Agarwal, Vyacheslav Mikhed and Barry Scholnick published a study using Canadian lottery data, and they found something remarkable. When someone in a neighborhood won the lottery, their close neighbors became more likely to borrow, and more likely to go bankrupt afterwards. The bigger the win, the stronger the effect. The neighbors hadn't won anything. They had just watched someone next door get richer, and some of them tried to keep up.

So here's the full domino chain. A random win creates sudden wealth. The brain files it as house money and spends it more freely. Spending creates permanent new costs and visible signs of wealth. Visible wealth changes how family, friends and even strangers treat the winner. Requests and pressure lead to suspicion and isolation. Isolation removes the people who might have slowed things down. And all the while, the money is quietly flowing out faster than anyone notices. No single step is a disaster. The disaster is the chain.

There is no better illustration of that chain than the story of Jack Whittaker.

Whittaker was not the stereotypical lottery winner. That's what makes his story so useful. Before he ever bought a ticket, he was the president of a construction company in West Virginia, and, according to accounts of his life, he was already worth more than seventeen million dollars. He was a businessman who understood contracts, payroll and risk. If anyone was prepared for sudden money, it should have been him.

On Christmas Day in 2002, Whittaker won a Powerball jackpot of three hundred and fourteen point nine million dollars, at the time the largest jackpot ever won on a single ticket in American lottery history. He chose the cash option, about a hundred and seventy million dollars before taxes, and walked away with roughly a hundred and thirteen million. There's the headline gap again: the famous number and the real number were very different things.

The beginning looked like the fairy tale. He pledged ten percent to Christian charities. He put around fourteen million dollars into a foundation that provided food and clothing to low-income families in rural West Virginia. He gave money to churches and to people in his community. In the early coverage, he looked like the ideal winner: generous, grounded, a man who already knew how to handle money.

The first warning sign came quickly, and it was about cash. In August 2003, while he was at a strip club, thieves took five hundred and forty-five thousand dollars in cash from his car. Authorities said he had been drugged. In January 2004, around two hundred thousand dollars was taken from his car again, and this time it was recovered. When he was asked why he carried so much cash, he gave an answer that was widely reported: "Because I can."

That answer is the psychology of house money in three words. A man who had spent his career managing money carefully was now treating it as something that could be carried around in a briefcase, displayed, and lost, because the amount was so large that losing half a million no longer registered as a real loss.

Then the chain reached his family. Whittaker was very close to his teenage granddaughter, Brandi Bragg, and he gave her a large allowance and a lot of money to spend. In September 2004, her friend Jesse Tribble, eighteen years old, was found dead in Whittaker's home. A coroner's report attributed his death to an overdose involving a mix of drugs. In December 2004, Brandi herself was found dead. Drugs were found in her system, but the official cause of death was listed as undetermined, and no one was charged. She was seventeen.

It would be wrong, and unfair, to say the lottery caused those deaths. Families lose children to drugs without ever winning anything. What the money did was remove every limit. It gave a teenager almost unlimited resources, and it surrounded the family with people who were drawn to those resources.

The legal and financial pressure kept building. Caesars in Atlantic City sued him over one and a half million dollars in checks that it said had bounced to cover gambling losses. A worker at a casino accused him of assault and sought damages, and in a filing in that case, Whittaker reportedly claimed that thieves had cleaned out his bank accounts. Whether he was actually broke is not clearly established in the public record; what's clear is that a man with more than a hundred million dollars was now, at least on paper, saying he couldn't pay. In 2009, his daughter Ginger, Brandi's mother, was found dead at her home; officials said they did not suspect foul play. In 2016, his home in Virginia burned down, and he said it was not insured. He died in 2020, at seventy-two, after a long illness.

What does this case actually prove? Not that lottery money is cursed. Whittaker had business skills, a large fortune before the win, and good intentions at the start. What it proves is that money is an amplifier, not a filter. It didn't install new weaknesses. It took the existing ones, the drinking, the gambling, the impulse to show and give and control, and it removed every friction that had kept them in check. Before the win, an expensive mistake had a natural limit. After the win, there was almost no limit at all.

So how has the system adapted to stories like his? In a few important ways.

First, there's anonymity. A growing number of U.S. states allow lottery winners to keep their identity private, and in some others, winners can claim the prize through a trust or a legal entity so their name isn't splashed across the news. That matters, because so much of the damage in the chain comes from visibility. If nobody knows you won, the requests, the pressure and the targeting never begin.

Second, there's the professional playbook. Lawyers and financial planners who work with sudden wealth tend to give the same advice: don't announce it, don't quit everything the same week, don't make big promises, assemble a team before you claim the money, and wait before making major decisions. All of this is an attempt to break the chain at its earliest links, before mental accounting and social pressure can take over.

Third, there's a choice most winners turn down: the annuity. Taking the prize as payments over decades sounds less exciting, and it can be worse on paper. But psychologically, it works like a lock. It turns house money back into something closer to a salary, and it puts a natural cap on how much damage a bad year can do.

So what does winning actually do to a person? It depends on which outcome you're looking at.

In the best case, the winner uses the money to remove one specific kind of stress, the constant financial worry, while keeping their routines, relationships and identity mostly intact. That's what the Swedish data suggests is possible: a lasting improvement in how people evaluate their lives, without a dramatic change in how their days feel.

In the realistic good case, which is probably the most common, people pay off what they owe, help family, buy a better house, and then gradually adapt. Five years later, they're more secure and somewhat more satisfied, but they're still recognizably the same person, with the same moods and the same frustrations, just without the bills.

In the realistic bad case, it looks like Florida. The money arrives, creates a lifestyle the person can't sustain, and buys a few years of relief before the same financial problems return, sometimes larger than before.

And in the failure case, it looks like Whittaker. The money doesn't just fail to solve problems; it multiplies them by removing every barrier that used to keep them small.

Now go back to where we started, with the two stories everyone knows: the fairy tale where money fixes everything, and the curse where money ruins everything. Both get the psychology wrong in the same way. Both treat money as something that does things to you. The research suggests it works almost the other way around. Money doesn't add happiness, and it doesn't add misery. It removes limits. It takes away the friction of not being able to afford things, and that friction, annoying as it is, was quietly shaping who you were and what you did.

For someone whose problems were mostly about money, removing that friction is a genuine gift. For someone whose problems were never really about money, it removes the last thing that was holding those problems in place.

A lottery win doesn't give you a new life. It gives your old life more power.
